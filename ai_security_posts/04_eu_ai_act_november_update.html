EU AI Act November Update - What Changed for Businesses

Updated: November 4, 2025 | Reading time: 13 minutes | Effective: November 1, 2025

Executive Summary

On November 1, 2025, the European Union's AI Act entered a critical new phase. The transition period ended, and several fundamental provisions became fully enforceable. This doesn't just affect technology companies—virtually every business using AI technology in any form (whether in internal processes or customer-facing) now faces compliance obligations.

With the November 1st deadline, new rules became mandatory in the following areas: immediate prohibition of banned AI practices, registration of high-risk AI systems, transparency obligations, and mandatory risk assessments. Penalties for violations are severe: up to 7% of annual global revenue or €35 million—whichever is higher.

This comprehensive guide is designed for businesses operating in the EU. It explains exactly what the November 1st changes mean, what specific obligations companies face, how member state authorities are interpreting the rules, and what SMEs and large enterprises must do immediately to ensure compliance.

What is the EU AI Act and Why is it Critical Now?

The EU AI Act (officially: Regulation on Artificial Intelligence) is the European Union's comprehensive legal framework for regulating artificial intelligence. Its goal is to ensure safe and trustworthy AI development and use while protecting fundamental rights and promoting innovation.

The AI Act's Risk-Based Approach

The regulation classifies AI systems into four risk categories:

Category Examples Obligations
Unacceptable risk Social scoring, real-time biometric identification in public spaces, manipulative AI COMPLETE BAN
High risk Critical infrastructure, employment, credit scoring, law enforcement, education Strict compliance requirements, registration, documentation, human oversight
Limited risk Chatbots, deepfake content, emotion recognition Transparency obligation - users must be informed they're interacting with AI
Minimal risk Spam filters, simple recommendation systems Voluntary best practices, no mandatory regulation

Why is November 1st the Critical Date?

The EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024, but with a phased implementation. The November 1, 2025 deadline marks the first major milestone when numerous fundamental provisions became mandatory:

  • The prohibition of banned AI practices became immediately enforceable
  • The compliance obligations for high-risk systems took effect
  • The transparency obligations became mandatory
  • The imposition of penalties can begin

From November 2025, companies can no longer reference a transition period—the rules are in force, and authorities are beginning to enforce them.

New Rules Effective November 1st

1. Banned AI Practices - Immediate Prohibition

AI applications completely prohibited from November 1st:

Subliminal manipulation: AI systems that manipulate people's behavior without their awareness. Example: hidden messages in websites that influence purchasing decisions.

Exploitation of vulnerable groups: AI systems that exploit vulnerabilities of children, elderly, or disabled persons. Example: games that manipulate children into unauthorized purchases.

Social scoring: Government AI systems that score citizens based on their social behavior. (Similar to Chinese systems).

Real-time biometric identification in public spaces: Facial recognition in public spaces in real-time, except for serious crimes with judicial authorization.

Emotion recognition in workplace and education: AI systems that analyze employees' or students' emotional states (except medical or security reasons).

IMPORTANT: If the company uses any such system, it must be immediately shut down from November 1st, under penalty and liability.

2. Obligations for High-Risk AI Systems

From November 1st, companies using or developing high-risk AI systems must fulfill the following obligations:

Risk assessment documentation:

  • Detailed risk analysis for every high-risk AI system
  • The system's purpose, operation, usage environment
  • Identified risks and mitigation measures
  • Expected impacts on fundamental rights

Technical documentation:

  • System design and development
  • Description of training data used
  • Algorithm operation (in understandable terms)
  • Performance metrics and testing results

Ensuring human oversight:

  • System cannot make decisions automatically without human intervention in high-risk areas
  • Designation of responsible person for oversight
  • Implementation of override mechanism

Data handling requirements:

  • Ensuring quality of training data
  • Identification and mitigation of biases
  • Compliance with data protection requirements (GDPR harmonization)

Transparency and information:

  • Informing affected parties that an AI system decided about them
  • Explaining the decision logic in understandable terms
  • Providing remedy options

Continuous monitoring and logging:

  • Automatic logging of AI system decisions
  • Regular performance evaluation
  • Incident reporting procedure for errors

3. Transparency Obligations - For All AI Users

From November 1st, every company using AI with customers must inform them:

Chatbots and virtual assistants: Must clearly indicate that the user is communicating with AI, not a human. Example: "This is an automated AI assistant" message in prominent location.

AI-generated content: If the company creates content with AI (text, images, videos, audio), this must be marked. Example: "This article was created with AI assistance" or "AI-generated image" watermark.

Deepfake content: Manipulated or synthetic media requires prominent warning. Example: "This video was modified/generated with AI".

Emotion and biometric recognition: If the company uses this (e.g., emotion analysis in customer service), prior information and consent are required.

4. General Purpose AI Models (GPAI) Special Rules

Large models like GPT-5, Gemini 2.5, Claude Opus 4.1 qualify as "General Purpose AI models" (GPAI). From November 1st, GPAI providers must:

  • Prepare public documentation on model capabilities and limitations
  • Describe training data sources (including copyright issues)
  • Publish energy consumption data
  • Assess and mitigate risks

For businesses: If they use ChatGPT, Claude, or Gemini, that's the providers' responsibility. But if the company fine-tunes or builds custom AI models, GPAI obligations may apply!

Penalty Levels and First Enforcement Cases

The EU AI Act's penalty system is severely deterrent. Fines are set as a percentage of annual global revenue:

Violation Type Fine Amount Or Fixed Amount
Using banned AI practice Global revenue 7% €35 million
High-risk AI compliance violation Global revenue 3% €15 million
Providing incorrect information to authorities Global revenue 1.5% €7.5 million
Transparency obligation violation Global revenue 1.5% €7.5 million

The higher amount is always imposed!

First Fines and Precedents - November 2025

Although only a few days have passed since November 1st, the European Commission has already indicated that member state authorities will exercise their powers. First cases are expected in the following categories:

Case Study #1: French recruitment company (preliminary procedure)

A French HR tech company used AI-based resume screening that automatically ranked candidates. The system didn't meet high-risk AI requirements: risk assessment documentation was missing, there was no human oversight, and candidates weren't informed. The French data protection authority (CNIL) initiated preliminary proceedings. Expected fine: €2-5 million.

Case Study #2: Italian e-commerce platform (ongoing)

An Italian webshop used a chatbot that didn't clearly indicate it was AI. Customers thought they were speaking with human customer service. Transparency obligation violation. The Italian authority (Garante) issued an official warning with a deadline for correction. Fine can be avoided if compliance achieved within 30 days.

Case Study #3: German insurance company (under investigation)

A German insurer conducted AI-based risk assessment for claims, automatically rejecting certain cases. The system qualifies as high-risk AI but lacked proper documentation and human override capability. The German data protection authority (BfDI) launched a detailed audit. Depending on outcome, €5-15 million fine expected.

What to Expect in Other EU Countries

Data protection authorities across the EU are preparing enforcement actions. First cases by end of 2025 are expected, particularly in these areas:

  • Banking credit scoring: AI-based credit assessment compliance checks
  • HR and recruitment: Resume screening and candidate evaluation system audits
  • Customer service chatbots: Transparency obligation compliance verification
  • Online advertising systems: Investigation of manipulative AI techniques

National Authority Interpretations

Each EU member state has designated a competent authority to enforce the AI Act. While the regulation is uniform, national interpretations and enforcement approaches vary.

Key National Authorities

Country Authority Enforcement Approach
Germany BfDI (Federal Data Protection) Strict, detailed audits
France CNIL Pragmatic, warnings before fines
Italy Garante Fast response, focused on transparency
Spain AEPD Collaborative, helps SMEs
Netherlands AP (Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens) Technical focus, AI expertise

Common Enforcement Priorities

Across EU authorities, the following priorities emerge for 2025-2026:

  1. Banned practices: Zero tolerance - immediate investigation and fines
  2. High-risk systems without documentation: High priority audits
  3. Transparency violations: Warnings first, then fines if not corrected
  4. GPAI providers: Scrutiny of large model providers (OpenAI, Google, Anthropic)

SME-Friendly Measures

Recognizing compliance challenges for SMEs, several authorities introduced relief measures:

  • Simplified documentation templates: Available on authority websites
  • Free consultation: Phone and email support for SMEs
  • 6-month "good faith compliance period": If company demonstrates work toward compliance, more lenient fines expected
  • Webinar series: Free online training for SMEs

Practical Compliance Guide for SMEs

Below is a step-by-step compliance guide for small and medium enterprises.

Step 1: AI Systems Inventory

What to do: Create a complete list of all AI technology the company uses.

Checklist questions:

  • Do we use chatbots on our website?
  • Do we have AI-based customer service systems?
  • Do we use AI for recruitment or HR evaluation?
  • Do we have AI-based decision-making in lending or insurance?
  • Do we use AI for marketing (personalized ads)?
  • Do we apply AI in production processes?
  • Do employees use ChatGPT, Claude, or other AI tools for work?

Documentation: Create a simple table:

AI System Name Provider Purpose User Base Personal Data
Website chatbot ChatBot Inc. Customer service All website visitors No
ChatGPT Enterprise OpenAI Content creation Marketing team (5 people) No
HR screening AI HireEasy Resume pre-screening HR department (2 people) Yes

Step 2: Risk Categorization

What to do: Classify every AI system into one of the 4 categories.

Decision tree:

1. Is it a banned practice?
   → Manipulation, social scoring, real-time facial recognition?
   → YES: IMMEDIATELY SHUT DOWN
   → NO: continue

2. Is it high-risk?
   → HR/recruitment, lending, law enforcement, critical infrastructure,
     educational assessment, insurance?
   → YES: High risk
   → NO: continue

3. Does it interact with people?
   → Chatbot, content generation, emotion analysis?
   → YES: Limited risk (transparency obligation)
   → NO: Minimal risk

Step 3: Compliance Measures - For High-Risk Systems

If you have high-risk AI, the following documents are mandatory:

A) Risk assessment documentation (sample structure):

  1. System description
    • What is the system's exact purpose?
    • How does it work? (code not needed, but flowchart yes)
    • What data does it use?
  2. Risk identification
    • What errors could occur?
    • What's the impact of an incorrect decision?
    • Could the system be discriminatory?
  3. Mitigation measures
    • Human oversight - who checks decisions?
    • Override capability - how to challenge a decision?
    • Regular testing - how often re-evaluate?
  4. Fundamental rights impact assessment
    • Does it affect privacy?
    • Is there discrimination risk?
    • Is remedy ensured?

B) Ensuring human oversight:

  • Designate a responsible person (doesn't need to be AI expert, but must understand system)
  • Tie every automatic decision to human approval
  • Monthly report: how many decisions, how many overrides, why

C) Informing affected parties:

  • Clearly state that an AI system decides about them
  • Explain how the system works (in understandable language)
  • Provide remedy option (email address, phone number)

Step 4: Compliance Measures - For Limited Risk

If you have limited-risk AI (e.g., chatbot):

Fulfilling transparency obligation:

  • For chatbots: Indicate in first message: "Hi! I'm an AI assistant. How can I help?"
  • For AI-generated content: Label: "This article was created with AI assistance" or "AI-generated image"
  • In email signatures: If AI helps with email responses, indicate: "This response was created with AI support"

Step 5: Documentation Storage and Maintenance

What to store and for how long:

  • Risk assessment documentation: 10 years
  • AI system decision logs: 6 months minimum (but 2 years recommended)
  • Human override documentation: 5 years
  • User information notices: 3 years from information date

Tip: Use dedicated folder or cloud-based document management (e.g., SharePoint, Google Drive), named "AI Compliance".

Step 6: Continuous Compliance

Regular tasks:

  • Monthly: Review AI decision logs, search for anomalies
  • Quarterly: Update risk assessment if system or usage changed
  • Semi-annually: Internal audit - all documents up to date?
  • Annually: Full compliance review, involving external expert (recommended)
